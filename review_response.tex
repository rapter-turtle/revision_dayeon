\section*{\normalsize{Reviewer 1}}

This manuscript proposes MSGAT, a metapath-based heterogeneous graph neural network for herb-phenotype association prediction. The method combines an incremental metapath-selection strategy with a sequential cross-type update mechanism, in which compound, herb, and phenotype representations are updated in a predefined order and newly updated representations are immediately used by subsequent node types. The topic is relevant to computational natural-product discovery, and the manuscript is generally clear. The large heterogeneous graph constructed from CODA and COCONUT and the Parkinson's disease case study are also potentially valuable. However, several methodological issues need to be addressed before the conclusions can be considered sufficiently supported. In particular, the current description of graph construction raises a serious concern regarding possible target-edge leakage; the proposed cross-type synchronization mechanism is not directly evaluated in the ablation study; the metapath-selection procedure requires a clearer separation between model selection and final evaluation; and stronger heterogeneous-graph baselines are needed.

\begin{revcomment}
\textbf{The train/validation/test protocol needs to be clarified.} The manuscript states that positive herb-phenotype edges are split into training, validation and test sets, and that a graph is generated for each split. Please clarify whether validation/test target edges are removed from the message-passing graph and from metapath construction. If target edges remain in the input graph, this would lead to information leakage.
\end{revcomment}

\section*{\normalsize{Reviewer 4 (Editorial Team)}}

The manuscript presents a Metapath-based Synchronized Graph Attention Network (MSGAT) for predicting herb--phenotype associations using heterogeneous biological networks. The proposed framework incorporates two main methodological components: incremental metapath optimization and cross-type synchronization of node embeddings.

However, several substantial methodological and experimental concerns must be addressed before the manuscript can be considered for publication. In particular, the graph construction and evaluation procedures raise questions about potential information leakage; the contribution of cross-type synchronization is not adequately isolated; the mathematical formulation of the link predictor appears insufficient for pair-specific association modeling; and the biological interpretation of the Parkinson's disease predictions is stronger than the presented evidence supports.

Dear Authors, You are welcome to incorporate any of my suggestions/recommendations (SEE ATTACHED FILE) that you feel will strengthen the manuscript. For comments that you believe fall outside the scope of the present study, please add a brief explanation in your rebuttal letter.

\setcommentlabel{Major Comment}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% R4 Major 1 %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{revcomment}
\textbf{Potential data leakage during graph construction and link prediction.}

One of my most significant concerns relates to how the heterogeneous graph is constructed and subsequently used for training, validation, and testing.

In Section 5.7, the authors state that positive edges are divided into training, validation, and test sets using a 6:2:2 ratio, after which separate graphs are generated for these sets and used as model inputs.

This description is ambiguous and potentially problematic because the model explicitly uses herb--phenotype associations as edges in the heterogeneous graph, while simultaneously attempting to predict those same associations.

Moreover, the selected metapaths include HP and PH, representing direct associations between herbs and phenotypes.

If a held-out herb--phenotype association remains in any graph or metapath-based adjacency matrix used to generate the embeddings for that prediction, the model may have direct access to the relationship it is supposed to predict. This would constitute target-edge leakage and could artificially inflate predictive performance.

I strongly recommend that the authors:
\begin{itemize}
\item Explicitly describe how graph adjacency matrices are constructed for each training, validation, and test fold.
\item Confirm that validation and test herb--phenotype edges, including their reverse-direction representations, are removed from all graph inputs before embedding generation.
\item Clarify whether metapath-based adjacency matrices are reconstructed separately for each fold using only permissible training edges.
\item Provide pseudocode describing the splitting, graph construction, negative sampling, and evaluation procedures.
\item Repeat the experiments using a strictly leakage-free evaluation protocol, if this has not already been implemented.
\end{itemize}

This issue is critical because unresolved information leakage would undermine the reliability of the reported performance.
\end{revcomment}

We thank the Reviewer for this important comment. Prompted by it, we re-examined our implementation and confirmed that the problem the Reviewer was concerned about did exist in the original implementation.

Reverse edges were handled correctly in the original implementation as well: a herb--phenotype (HP) edge and its reverse phenotype--herb (PH) edge share the same edge ID and were always removed together. However, when separate graphs were built for each split, the validation graph retained the validation HP edges and the test graph retained the test HP edges, and the embeddings of each split were computed on that graph. As a result, the target edges were exposed at evaluation time to message passing and to the metapaths containing the HP and PH relations (PH, HP and HPH). Because validation performance was also used for early stopping and metapath selection, this problem may have affected model selection as well as the reported performance values.

We corrected the evaluation protocol so that validation and test edges are not included in any graph used for embedding generation, and we repeated all experiments under the corrected protocol. All performance values in the manuscript have accordingly been replaced with the new results. We address the five requests in turn below.

\textbf{(1) Construction of graph adjacency matrices for each split.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The graph consists of three node types (compound, herb and phenotype) and five relations, each with its reverse: compound--compound, compound--phenotype, phenotype--phenotype, herb--compound and herb--phenotype.
\item Only the HP edges, which are the prediction targets, are divided into training, validation and test sets (\TODO{6:2:2}). The other relations are not prediction targets and are used identically at all stages.
\item The split is made at random at the level of HP edges. A reverse PH edge has the same edge ID as its forward edge, so it always belongs to the same set. \TODO{Keep or delete after the duplicate check:} Herb--phenotype pairs recorded more than once in the data were merged before splitting, so that the same pair is never divided across different sets.
\item In the corrected protocol, separate graphs are no longer built for each split. We build a single training graph ($G_{train}$) by removing the validation and test HP edges and their reverse PH edges from the full graph, and the embeddings for training, validation and testing are all computed on $G_{train}$. Validation and test edges are used only as targets to be scored.
\item The way in which the split is repeated is explained in the response to Minor Comment 2.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{(2) Removal of validation and test HP edges and their reverse edges from all graph inputs.} Yes, in the corrected protocol they are all removed. In addition, each run automatically checks whether any validation or test herb--phenotype pair remains in $G_{train}$ in the forward (HP) or reverse (PH) direction, and the run stops if one is found. All reported experiments passed this check.

\textbf{(3) Reconstruction of metapath-based adjacency matrices using only training edges.} Yes. Metapath-based adjacency matrices are not taken from stored matrices; they are computed from the graph given to the model. Metapaths containing the HP or PH relation (\TODO{PH, HP, HPH}) are recomputed whenever the input graph changes, that is, when edges are masked during training and at evaluation, so they are built only from training edges as long as the input is $G_{train}$. Metapaths without the HP or PH relation (\TODO{CH, CC, HC, PP, PCP}) do not depend on the split and are computed once and reused. All metapath-based neighbors therefore derive only from permissible training edges.

\begin{revcomment}
\textbf{The mathematical formulation of the link predictor may not capture herb--phenotype-specific interactions.}

The manuscript defines the association prediction function as:
\[ p_{uv} = \sigma\left( W_l^\top [z'_u \,\|\, z'_v] + b_l \right). \]
Assuming Equation 14 describes a single affine transformation followed by a sigmoid, the score can be rewritten as:
\[ p_{uv} = \sigma\left( w_H^\top z'_u + w_P^\top z'_v + b_l \right). \]
This is an important limitation because the resulting score is additively separable into herb-specific and phenotype-specific contributions.

Consequently, once the node embeddings are fixed, the relative ranking of herbs would be identical for every phenotype. The predictor would not directly model pairwise compatibility between a particular herb and a particular phenotype.

For a method specifically designed to identify biologically meaningful herb--phenotype associations, this limitation is particularly concerning.

The authors should clarify whether Equation 14 accurately represents their implementation. Figure 1 depicts an MLP-based predictor, which may imply a more expressive architecture than the mathematical formulation currently describes.

If the implemented decoder is indeed a single affine layer, I recommend evaluating alternative association functions that explicitly model interactions, such as:
\[ p_{uv} = \sigma\left( z'^{\top}_u W z'_v + b \right), \]
or a multilayer perceptron containing nonlinear hidden layers.

An ablation comparing additive, bilinear, dot-product, and nonlinear MLP decoders would help establish whether the reported performance reflects meaningful herb--phenotype compatibility or primarily node-level association propensities.

This concern also has direct implications for the biological specificity of the Parkinson's disease case study.
\end{revcomment}

\begin{revcomment}
\textbf{Insufficient ablation experiments to establish the contribution of cross-type synchronization.}

Cross-type synchronization is presented as one of the primary methodological innovations of MSGAT. However, the reported experiments do not adequately isolate its contribution.

The current ablation study (Table 2) examines the effects of residual connections and layer normalization. Although these components are relevant to the proposed architecture, their removal does not directly demonstrate whether sequential cross-type synchronization is beneficial.

\begin{revcomment}
\textbf{Incremental metapath optimization requires stronger methodological and statistical justification.}

The proposed incremental metapath optimization strategy is interesting. However, the procedure resembles a greedy forward-selection algorithm rather than a method that necessarily identifies an optimal metapath set.

The authors begin with CH, CC, and PP metapaths and sequentially incorporate candidates based on paired t-tests. This introduces several methodological concerns.

First, the authors should clarify whether metapath selection is performed exclusively on training/validation data within each cross-validation fold. If performance on the test folds influences metapath selection, test-set information may inadvertently enter model development.

Second, the repeated use of paired t-tests at an unadjusted significance level of 0.05 across multiple candidates and iterations introduces a multiple-comparison problem. With only five paired fold results, uncertainty in the estimated improvements also requires careful treatment.

Third, greedy selection does not guarantee a globally optimal metapath set because different metapaths may exhibit redundancy or complementary effects.

Finally, the base metapaths and maximum metapath length of three are still chosen using prior assumptions. Therefore, the assertion that the approach eliminates knowledge-based metapath selection is too strong.

I recommend that the authors report the complete metapath-selection trajectory, including candidate sets, performance increments, adjusted significance levels, and computational costs. Nested validation or another independent model-selection procedure would be preferable.

The selection strategy should also be compared against predefined biologically motivated metapaths, random metapath selection, and alternative greedy or attention-based strategies.
\end{revcomment}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% R4 Major 5 %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{revcomment}
\textbf{Negative sampling and evaluation design may not reflect real-world association discovery.}

The authors employ weighted negative sampling, generating an equal number of positive and negative herb--phenotype pairs. Although this approach is convenient for supervised model training and comparisons, it raises two important concerns.

First, unobserved herb--phenotype associations are not necessarily true negatives. Some may represent biologically plausible but currently undocumented relationships. Treating all sampled unknown associations as negatives may introduce mislabeled examples.

Second, evaluating performance on an artificially balanced dataset does not adequately reflect the extreme sparsity of real-world herb--phenotype association discovery.

For perspective, the graph contains approximately 434,686 recorded herb--phenotype associations among 30,230 herbs and 108,815 phenotypes. This represents only about 0.013\% of all possible herb--phenotype pairs, although the remaining unobserved pairs should not be interpreted as confirmed biological negatives.

Consequently, the reported AUPRC of 0.7240 is measured under an evaluation prevalence that differs substantially from the intended discovery setting.

I recommend evaluating the model using several additional protocols, including realistic candidate pools, alternative negative-sampling strategies, and positive-unlabeled evaluation where appropriate.

Particularly useful ranking metrics would include Precision@K, Recall@K, Hits@K, and enrichment among top-ranked predictions. Comparisons with node-degree and association-frequency baselines are also important because graph models can exploit highly connected nodes.

The authors should additionally clarify whether negative-sampling frequencies are estimated exclusively from training data, whether negative pairs are fixed during testing, and whether sampled negatives overlap with known positive edges in other splits.
\end{revcomment}

\begin{revcomment}
\textbf{Limited biological interpretability and incomplete modeling of the multi-compound multi-target problem.}

The manuscript positions MSGAT as a framework for addressing the multi-compound multi-target (MCMT) complexity of natural products.

However, the constructed heterogeneous network contains only herb, compound, and phenotype nodes. Explicit molecular targets, such as proteins, genes, and biological pathways, are not represented.

Consequently, although MSGAT can capture higher-order associations among herbs, compounds, and phenotypes, it does not directly model compound--target interactions or pharmacological synergy.

This distinction should be clarified because the paper's biological motivation extends beyond the mechanisms actually represented by the graph.

Another issue concerns the interpretation of edges. An herb--phenotype association does not necessarily imply a beneficial therapeutic effect. Depending on the underlying database, such an association could represent a treatment relationship, an observed biological effect, a causal relationship, or another form of association.

I recommend that the authors describe the semantic meaning and direction of each edge type and explain how beneficial and adverse associations are distinguished, if at all.

The biological interpretability of MSGAT could also be improved through analyses that identify which compounds, metapaths, or graph neighborhoods contribute most to individual predictions. Such analyses would provide more meaningful mechanistic support than global prediction scores alone.

\begin{revcomment}
\textbf{Reproducibility, random node features, and generalizability require further attention.}

The authors initialize all node representations as 1,024-dimensional vectors sampled from a standard normal distribution.

This design raises questions about the model's dependence on learned node identities rather than biologically meaningful intrinsic features.
